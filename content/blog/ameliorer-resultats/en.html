On TikTok Shop, creators make the sales. In France, affiliates generated 73.4% of the platform's revenue over the 90 days to 9 July 2026, and short video carried 61.6% of it, against 20.2% for live (Kalodata estimates published by Lengow). Across the four largest European markets, affiliates accounted for 69.9%. A creator's results therefore depend on levers they control. There are five, and the order you work on them matters as much as the levers themselves.

Which lever should you start with?

The one that is blocking. Before changing anything, find where your videos lose the most people (the method is in how to analyse your TikTok Shop videos), then use this table:

Symptom in your statisticsPriority lever
Few viewers still there at 3 seconds1. The hook
Good retention, many clicks, few orders2. The offer
A few videos that work, then nothing3. The rhythm
Good retention, few product clicks4. The call to action
Irregular results with no explanation5. The data

Lever 1: how does the hook change sales?

The hook decides how many people will see your sales pitch. TikTok Shop Academy places it in the first 3 to 6 seconds and recommends tying it to the featured product (PPC Land). A Buffer study of 1.1 million videos measured a median watch time of 6.9 seconds for 30-to-60-second videos (Buffer): anything after that point is seen by a minority.

Concrete action: product visible from the first frame, promise written on screen, no introduction. The full method is in how to analyse your TikTok Shop hooks.

Lever 2: is the product or the offer the problem?

A great video won't save a bad offer. If viewers click but don't buy, the product page breaks the promise: a higher price than expected, weak reviews, delivery times, disappointing visuals.

Some reference points from the French market: over the 30 days to 9 July 2026, the average price of items sold by the ten largest shops was €46.26, and beauty and personal care (18.3% of national revenue) and household appliances (12.5%) were the top two categories (Lengow). These aren't rules, but a product priced well above that needs a longer demonstration and more proof.

  • Pick products whose benefit can be seen in a few seconds.
  • Read the product page reviews before promoting: if buyers complain, your viewers will notice too.
  • Compare commissions, but never pick a product on that alone: a high commission on a product that doesn't convert pays nothing.

Lever 3: how often should you post?

Consistency matters more than volume. Each video is a test: the more regularly you post, the faster you learn what works in your niche. But posting lots of weak videos doesn't help; three well-made videos a week teach you more than ten rushed ones, because they produce differences you can read.

Think about format too. In France, live accounted for 20.2% of revenue over the period studied by Lengow: not the main channel, but a serious complement once your videos have built an audience that trusts you. As for length, Buffer's study concludes that video length on its own is unlikely to be decisive: fit it to the time needed to demonstrate the product.

Lever 4: how should you phrase the call to action?

TikTok Shop Academy puts it simply: every video should end with a clear next step, spoken or written on screen, because many viewers watch without sound (PPC Land). The three most common mistakes:

  1. A call that comes too late: placed after retention drops, almost nobody sees it. Say it once right after the demonstration.
  2. A vague call: "go get it" doesn't say what to do. "Tap the product link below the video" does.
  3. A call with no reason: restate the main benefit when you ask for the click.

Lever 5: how do you use your data to improve?

The last lever governs the other four. Without reading your statistics regularly, you change things at random. The minimum routine:

  • every week, rank your videos by orders, not views;
  • compare your best and worst video level by level (hook, retention, click, conversion);
  • test one change on the next videos;
  • wait 48 to 72 hours before concluding, so distribution has time to play out.

Key takeaway: the five levers aren't worked on at the same time. One project at a time, chosen from your numbers, produces measurable progress. Five in parallel produce noise.
